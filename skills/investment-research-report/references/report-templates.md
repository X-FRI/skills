# Report Templates

Use this reference before drafting any investment research output.

## Short Note Template

Use when the user asks for a concise note or quick framework.

1. Research disclaimer.
2. Executive summary: object, stance, horizon, return source, mispricing, key risk, verification data.
3. Research object and question.
4. Core thesis: 3-5 assumptions with validation and disconfirmation data.
5. Industry, competition, and return source.
6. Valuation and market-implied expectations.
7. Catalysts.
8. Investment plan.
9. Risks and falsification checklist.
10. Tracking plan.

## Full Report Template

Use when the user asks for a complete report.

1. Research disclaimer.
2. Executive summary.
3. Research object and investment question.
4. Investment horizon.
5. Background that serves the thesis.
6. Core thesis and key assumptions.
7. Validation indicators.
8. Growth versus shareholder return.
9. Competitive landscape.
10. Valuation and market-implied expectations.
11. Market mispricing.
12. Catalysts.
13. Investment plan.
14. Risk analysis.
15. Falsification checklist.
16. Tracking and review plan.

## Section Requirements

### Executive Summary

Start with the conclusion, not background. Include:

- Research object.
- Stance: bullish, bearish, neutral, or watchlist-only.
- Investment horizon.
- Expected return source.
- Market mispricing or expectation gap.
- Biggest risk.
- Most important evidence that would strengthen or weaken the thesis.

Avoid generic openings like "the industry has broad prospects" or "policy support is strong". Replace them with a specific, falsifiable investment claim.

### Research Object and Investment Question

Define whether the report studies a company, industry, sub-sector, value-chain segment, theme, ETF, basket, or factor exposure.

State a testable investment question. Good examples:

- "Is current valuation already pricing in domestic substitution?"
- "Is sector weakness structural decline or a valuation-cycle opportunity?"
- "Can this company convert revenue growth into free cash flow and shareholder return?"
- "Will margin recovery be visible within the next two quarters?"

Avoid vague questions like "Is this industry good?" or "Will this stock rise?"

### Investment Horizon

State the intended horizon before building the thesis.

- Short-term theme trade: expectation gap, catalysts, liquidity, policy events, sentiment, positioning, and valuation elasticity.
- 6-12 month fundamental trade: orders, pricing, inventory, earnings revisions, margin, cash flow, and valuation repair.
- 3-5 year structural investment: penetration, business model, competitive advantage, ROIC, capital allocation, and free cash flow.

Do not use a long-term story to justify a short-term trade unless the near-term catalyst is explicit.

### Background That Serves the Thesis

Keep background concise and decision-useful. Explain:

- Industry life cycle and recent change.
- Demand drivers and supply constraints.
- Pricing power and profit-pool location.
- Revenue, profit, cash-flow, and balance-sheet quality.
- What the market already knows.

The point is not to prove the industry is large or important. The point is whether the asset can generate shareholder return at the current valuation.

### Core Thesis and Key Assumptions

Express the thesis as 3-5 testable assumptions. For each assumption, specify:

- What is assumed.
- Why it might be true.
- Current supporting evidence.
- Data that would verify it.
- Data that would disprove it.

Do not write broad claims such as "demand is strong", "policy is favorable", or "valuation will repair" without defining measurable meaning.

### Validation Indicators

Every thesis needs measurable indicators. Select only indicators that map to the assumptions.

Common indicators:

- Revenue, orders, backlog, pricing, market share.
- Gross margin, net margin, operating cash flow, free cash flow.
- Receivables turnover, inventory turnover, capex, utilization.
- Tender, procurement, export, policy, or channel data.
- Management guidance, earnings revisions, valuation percentile, fund positioning.

Avoid narrative-only indicators such as "market recognition", "industry trend", or "long-term potential".

### Growth Versus Shareholder Return

Distinguish industry growth from investor return. Growth can fail to reach shareholders because of price competition, overcapacity, capital expenditure, receivables deterioration, dilution, or valuation compression.

Identify likely return source:

- Earnings growth.
- Margin recovery.
- Valuation expansion.
- Dividend yield or buybacks.
- Free cash flow improvement.
- Asset revaluation.
- Cycle recovery.
- Market share concentration.

Explain whether the return source is already priced in.

### Competitive Landscape

Competition determines who earns the profit. Analyze the relevant subset:

- Concentration and leader share trend.
- Entry barriers, technology risk, substitution risk.
- Customer stickiness, channel control, cost advantage, scale advantage.
- Pricing power, regulatory barriers, capital intensity.

For value-chain research, identify where profit is captured. The best exposure may be upstream resources, key equipment, core materials, software, distribution, testing, or operations rather than the most visible theme stock.
