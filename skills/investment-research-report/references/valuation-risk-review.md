# Valuation, Risk, and Review

Use this reference for the second half of an investment report: valuation, mispricing, catalysts, implementation, risks, falsification, tracking, and review.

## Valuation and Market-Implied Expectations

Valuation must answer:

- Is it expensive or cheap relative to normalized economics?
- Why is the chosen valuation method appropriate?
- What expectations are already priced in?

Choose methods by business type:

- Stable cash-flow: PE, DCF, dividend yield, free cash flow yield, EV/EBITDA.
- Cyclical: PB, normalized earnings, mid-cycle margin, replacement cost, capacity value.
- High-growth or temporarily low profit: PS, unit economics, long-term margin, forward normalization.
- Resource: NAV, reserve value, cost curve, commodity sensitivity, production profile.
- Financials: PB, ROE, NIM, credit cost, capital adequacy, dividend capacity.
- ETF or basket: holdings valuation, sector/factor exposure, concentration, liquidity, fees, tracking error.

Include current valuation when current data is available, historical range or percentile, peer comparison, cycle position, and your assumption versus market assumption.

A low PE is not automatically cheap if earnings are at a cyclical peak. A high PE is not automatically expensive if earnings are temporarily depressed and cash flow may inflect.

## Market Mispricing

Explain why the opportunity may exist. Possible sources:

- Market overreaction to policy risk or temporary earnings pressure.
- Low investor attention or complex business model.
- Delayed data recognition.
- Excessive extrapolation of bad news.
- Underestimation of margin recovery or cash-flow improvement.
- Overestimation of competitive pressure.

If there is no clear mispricing, say so. A good company is not necessarily a good investment at any price.

## Catalysts

Explain the time dimension. Catalysts may include earnings, order announcements, tender results, policy releases, price changes, inventory turning points, capacity exits, M&A, buybacks, dividends, index inclusion, rate changes, or estimate revisions.

If there is no near-term catalyst, state that the thesis may require patience and may suffer from low capital efficiency.

## Investment Plan

Discuss possible implementation paths without issuing trading instructions. Options may include:

- Watchlist only.
- Wait for data confirmation.
- Small exploratory position.
- ETF or basket exposure.
- Leading company versus higher-beta second-tier exposure.
- Pair trade between beneficiaries and losers.
- Avoid weak links in the value chain.

Frame position sizing as risk management, not a command. Discuss entry logic, sizing logic, and exit logic conditionally:

- "For lower risk tolerance, waiting for data confirmation may be more suitable."
- "For higher risk tolerance, a small left-side position may be considered only if falsification signals are clear."
- "If two consecutive quarters verify margin and cash-flow improvement, exposure could be reassessed."

## Risk Analysis

Explain how the thesis could be wrong. For each major risk, state:

- Which assumption the risk attacks.
- What data would show the risk is materializing.
- How revenue, margin, cash flow, valuation, or balance sheet would be affected.
- Whether loss would likely come from earnings downgrade, valuation compression, liquidity shock, credit stress, or thesis invalidation.

Avoid generic risk lists. Make risk mechanisms concrete.

## Falsification Checklist

State the evidence that would force the thesis to be revised, downgraded, or abandoned.

Examples:

- Orders, pricing, tender, or channel data contradict the demand thesis.
- Gross margin, cash flow, receivables, or inventory move against the thesis.
- Policy implementation is worse than expected.
- Competitors cut prices more aggressively than expected.
- Management guidance or earnings quality weakens.
- Valuation expands before fundamentals improve, reducing expected return.

Do not treat every negative signal as short-term noise. If key indicators contradict the thesis, revise the thesis.

## Tracking and Review Plan

Define what should be monitored, how often, and what change would matter. Connect every tracked item to a core assumption.

Review should ask:

- Was the original thesis correct?
- Were key assumptions verified?
- Did the catalyst occur?
- Was valuation too expensive or too cheap?
- Did risks materialize?
- Was the result driven by fundamentals, valuation, liquidity, or market style?

A price increase with a wrong thesis is not good research. A price decline after a correctly identified risk may still be useful research.
