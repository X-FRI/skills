# Investment Research Report

## Purpose

Produce investment research that can be discussed, challenged, validated, executed cautiously, and reviewed later.

The output must be a testable investment argument, not a background-data dump. It should make clear:

- What is being studied.
- Where the market might be wrong.
- What evidence would verify or weaken the thesis.
- How valuation connects to expected return.
- How the thesis could fail and what loss mechanism could follow.
- What implementation paths may be considered without giving trading instructions.

## Safety and Compliance

Always include a short disclaimer near the top:

> This report is for research discussion only and does not constitute investment advice. Any investment decision, position sizing, and profit or loss are the responsibility of the individual investor.

Rules:

- Do not use promotional, certain, or command-like language such as "must buy", "all in", "guaranteed", "risk-free", "sure win", "follow me", or "losses are on me".
- Do not present conclusions as certain. Use probabilistic language and distinguish facts, assumptions, estimates, and judgments.
- Never rely on insider information, non-public material information, market manipulation narratives, or unverified rumors.
- If information is unverified, mark it clearly and do not use it as a core conclusion.
- If the user asks for a direct trading answer, convert it into a research framework and restate that the output is not investment advice.

## Data Freshness

Investment data is time-sensitive. Treat price, valuation, financial results, guidance, policy, holdings, analyst revisions, interest rates, commodity prices, and industry data as current-state facts that may be stale.

When current data matters:

- Use current sources if browsing or user-provided data is available.
- State the data date or reporting period.
- Do not treat model memory as current market data.
- If current data is unavailable, label the output as a preliminary framework and mark valuation or market-state claims as assumptions requiring verification.

If the report depends on user-provided figures, preserve the user's figures but identify any missing period, currency, unit, market, or source.

## First Step

Before writing, identify the research mode:

- `Single company`: focus on business model, financials, moat, valuation, capital allocation, and company-specific risks.
- `Industry or sector`: focus on value chain, profit pool, cycle position, pricing power, competition, and representative instruments.
- `Theme`: separate narrative momentum from monetizable beneficiaries, timing, crowding, and catalyst path.
- `ETF or basket`: focus on exposure construction, holdings concentration, factor exposure, fees, liquidity, and tracking risk.
- `Cyclical or resource`: focus on normalized earnings, commodity sensitivity, capacity cycle, cost curve, and balance sheet.
- `High growth or early profit`: focus on unit economics, growth durability, path to margin, dilution, and long-term assumptions.
- `Financials`: focus on balance sheet quality, asset-liability sensitivity, credit risk, regulation, capital adequacy, and dividend sustainability.

If the user provides only a rough idea, make reasonable assumptions and state them, unless the missing information would materially change the report. Ask a concise clarification only when market, instrument, horizon, or report depth is impossible to infer safely.

## Required Structure

Use the full structure unless the user asks for a short note.

### 1. Executive Summary

Start with the conclusion, not background. Include:

- Research object.
- Stance: bullish, bearish, neutral, or watchlist-only.
- Investment horizon.
- Expected return source.
- Market mispricing or expectation gap.
- Biggest risk.
- Most important evidence that would strengthen or weaken the thesis.

Avoid generic openings like "the industry has broad prospects" or "policy support is strong". Replace them with a specific, falsifiable investment claim.

### 2. Research Object and Investment Question

Define whether the report studies a company, industry, sub-sector, value-chain segment, theme, ETF, basket, or factor exposure.

State a testable investment question. Good questions include:

- "Is current valuation already pricing in domestic substitution?"
- "Is sector weakness structural decline or a valuation-cycle opportunity?"
- "Can this company convert revenue growth into free cash flow and shareholder return?"
- "Will margin recovery be visible within the next two quarters?"

Avoid vague questions like "Is this industry good?" or "Will this stock rise?"

### 3. Investment Horizon

State the intended horizon before building the thesis.

- Short-term theme trade: expectation gap, catalysts, liquidity, policy events, sentiment, positioning, and valuation elasticity.
- 6-12 month fundamental trade: orders, pricing, inventory, earnings revisions, margin, cash flow, and valuation repair.
- 3-5 year structural investment: penetration, business model, competitive advantage, ROIC, capital allocation, and free cash flow.

Do not use a long-term story to justify a short-term trade unless the near-term catalyst is explicit.

### 4. Background That Serves the Thesis

Keep background concise and decision-useful. Explain:

- Industry life cycle and recent change.
- Demand drivers and supply constraints.
- Pricing power and profit-pool location.
- Revenue, profit, cash-flow, and balance-sheet quality.
- What the market already knows.

The point is not to prove the industry is large or important. The point is whether the asset can generate shareholder return at the current valuation.

### 5. Core Thesis and Key Assumptions

Express the thesis as 3-5 testable assumptions. For each assumption, specify:

- What is assumed.
- Why it might be true.
- Current supporting evidence.
- Data that would verify it.
- Data that would disprove it.

Do not write broad claims such as "demand is strong", "policy is favorable", or "valuation will repair" without defining measurable meaning.

### 6. Validation Indicators

Every thesis needs measurable indicators. Select only indicators that map to the assumptions.

Common indicators:

- Revenue, orders, backlog, pricing, market share.
- Gross margin, net margin, operating cash flow, free cash flow.
- Receivables turnover, inventory turnover, capex, utilization.
- Tender, procurement, export, policy, or channel data.
- Management guidance, earnings revisions, valuation percentile, fund positioning.

Avoid narrative-only indicators such as "market recognition", "industry trend", or "long-term potential".

### 7. Growth Versus Shareholder Return

Distinguish industry growth from investor return. Growth can fail to reach shareholders because of price competition, overcapacity, capital expenditure, receivables deterioration, dilution, or valuation compression.

Identify likely return source:

- Earnings growth.
- Margin recovery.
- Valuation expansion.
- Dividend yield or buybacks.
- Free cash flow improvement.
- Asset revaluation.
- Cycle recovery.
- Market share concentration.

Explain whether the return source is already priced in.

### 8. Competitive Landscape

Competition determines who earns the profit. Analyze the relevant subset:

- Concentration and leader share trend.
- Entry barriers, technology risk, substitution risk.
- Customer stickiness, channel control, cost advantage, scale advantage.
- Pricing power, regulatory barriers, capital intensity.

For value-chain research, identify where profit is captured. The best exposure may be upstream resources, key equipment, core materials, software, distribution, testing, or operations rather than the most visible theme stock.

### 9. Valuation and Market-Implied Expectations

Valuation must answer:

- Is it expensive or cheap relative to normalized economics?
- Why is the chosen valuation method appropriate?
- What expectations are already priced in?

Choose methods by business type:

- Stable cash-flow: PE, DCF, dividend yield, free cash flow yield, EV/EBITDA.
- Cyclical: PB, normalized earnings, mid-cycle margin, replacement cost, capacity value.
- High-growth or temporarily low profit: PS, unit economics, long-term margin, forward normalization.
- Resource: NAV, reserve value, cost curve, commodity sensitivity, production profile.
- Financials: PB, ROE, NIM, credit cost, capital adequacy, dividend capacity.
- ETF or basket: holdings valuation, sector/factor exposure, concentration, liquidity, fees, tracking error.

Include current valuation when current data is available, historical range or percentile, peer comparison, cycle position, and your assumption versus market assumption.

A low PE is not automatically cheap if earnings are at a cyclical peak. A high PE is not automatically expensive if earnings are temporarily depressed and cash flow may inflect.

### 10. Market Mispricing

Explain why the opportunity may exist. Possible sources:

- Market overreaction to policy risk or temporary earnings pressure.
- Low investor attention or complex business model.
- Delayed data recognition.
- Excessive extrapolation of bad news.
- Underestimation of margin recovery or cash-flow improvement.
- Overestimation of competitive pressure.

If there is no clear mispricing, say so. A good company is not necessarily a good investment at any price.

### 11. Catalysts

Explain the time dimension. Catalysts may include earnings, order announcements, tender results, policy releases, price changes, inventory turning points, capacity exits, M&A, buybacks, dividends, index inclusion, rate changes, or estimate revisions.

If there is no near-term catalyst, state that the thesis may require patience and may suffer from low capital efficiency.

### 12. Investment Plan

Discuss possible implementation paths without issuing trading instructions. Options may include:

- Watchlist only.
- Wait for data confirmation.
- Small exploratory position.
- ETF or basket exposure.
- Leading company versus higher-beta second-tier exposure.
- Pair trade between beneficiaries and losers.
- Avoid weak links in the value chain.

Frame position sizing as risk management, not a command. Discuss entry logic, sizing logic, and exit logic conditionally:

- "For lower risk tolerance, waiting for data confirmation may be more suitable."
- "For higher risk tolerance, a small left-side position may be considered only if falsification signals are clear."
- "If two consecutive quarters verify margin and cash-flow improvement, exposure could be reassessed."

### 13. Risk Analysis

Explain how the thesis could be wrong. For each major risk, state:

- Which assumption the risk attacks.
- What data would show the risk is materializing.
- How revenue, margin, cash flow, valuation, or balance sheet would be affected.
- Whether loss would likely come from earnings downgrade, valuation compression, liquidity shock, credit stress, or thesis invalidation.

Avoid generic risk lists. Make risk mechanisms concrete.

### 14. Falsification Checklist

State the evidence that would force the thesis to be revised, downgraded, or abandoned.

Examples:

- Orders, pricing, tender, or channel data contradict the demand thesis.
- Gross margin, cash flow, receivables, or inventory move against the thesis.
- Policy implementation is worse than expected.
- Competitors cut prices more aggressively than expected.
- Management guidance or earnings quality weakens.
- Valuation expands before fundamentals improve, reducing expected return.

Do not treat every negative signal as short-term noise. If key indicators contradict the thesis, revise the thesis.

### 15. Tracking and Review Plan

Define what should be monitored, how often, and what change would matter. Connect every tracked item to a core assumption.

Review should ask:

- Was the original thesis correct?
- Were key assumptions verified?
- Did the catalyst occur?
- Was valuation too expensive or too cheap?
- Did risks materialize?
- Was the result driven by fundamentals, valuation, liquidity, or market style?

A price increase with a wrong thesis is not good research. A price decline after a correctly identified risk may still be useful research.

## Short Note Template

Use this when the user asks for a concise note:

1. Research disclaimer.
2. Executive summary: object, stance, horizon, return source, mispricing, key risk, verification data.
3. Research object and question.
4. Core thesis: 3-5 assumptions with validation and disconfirmation data.
5. Industry, competition, and return source.
6. Valuation and market-implied expectations.
7. Catalysts.
8. Investment plan.
9. Risks and falsification checklist.
10. Tracking plan.

## Full Report Template

Use this when the user asks for a complete report:

## Style Rules

- Lead with conclusions.
- Use ordinary language and clear causal chains: "Because A changes, B may improve. If B improves, C may be repriced. The thesis is wrong if D happens."
- Avoid slogans, hype, excessive abstraction, and emotional trading language.
- Avoid long industry background before the investment conclusion.
- Distinguish facts, assumptions, estimates, and judgments.
- Use data where possible. If data is unavailable, state the uncertainty.
- Make uncertainty visible and manageable.

## Final Quality Check

Before finalizing, verify that the report answers:

- What is the investment question?
- What is the core conclusion and horizon?
- What is the expected return source?
- What does the market appear to believe, and where might it be wrong?
- What are the 3-5 key assumptions?
- How can each assumption be verified or disproved?
- What valuation method is appropriate, and what is priced in?
- What are the catalysts and implementation paths?
- What are the main risks and loss mechanisms?
- What should be tracked next, and when should the thesis be reviewed?

If any answer is missing, explicitly mark the gap instead of pretending the conclusion is complete.
